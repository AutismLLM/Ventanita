VENTANITA — THE DECK

We have nothing to sell. That's the pitch. Read it twice.

SLIDE 1 — THE PROBLEM
A cook cannot type and flip tortillas at the same time. This is the entire problem. Every billion-dollar "conversational commerce" company on earth was founded to solve this one sentence, and every single one of them forgot the tortilla. They built cloud-native, SOC2-compliant, event-driven, serverless, AI-orchestrated toll booths on a road that was already free, then charged the cook $500 a month to walk through them. We did not build a toll booth. We built a door. The door was always there. It is labeled "your own computer." Nobody noticed because the door does not have a Series A.

SLIDE 2 — THE SOLUTION
A while-loop. That is the product. There is no rest of the product. Badge goes up, click chat, read text, think, type reply, loop. You have now read the entire architecture. There are no microservices warming up in us-east-1. There is no Chief AI Officer presenting a slide titled "TAM Expansion Through Vertical Integration of Messaging Adjacencies." There is a cook who finally gets to eat lunch. Guess which one solves the problem.

SLIDE 3 — THE MARKET
$4.7 billion. That is how much the industry charges cooks for a while-loop. We are not entering this market. We are deleting it. Not by competing, not by undercutting, by making the emperor's new clothes visible to everyone with 300 lines of Python and a SQLite file with three columns. When the solution is this obvious, the market does not shrink. It evaporates. You cannot charge rent on a room the tenant already owns.

SLIDE 4 — THE TECHNOLOGY
Boring on purpose. OCR from 2005. Mouse events from 1998. A database you can open with Notepad. An LLM rented by the token for cents, sitting in one small deterministic box so everything around it stays debuggable at 2 a.m. during a dinner rush. We considered adding seventeen boxes connected by arrows labeled "event bus" and "orchestration layer." We deleted them. They were lies. The only honest diagram has nine steps and fits on a napkin. The WiFi password in the kitchen is also on a napkin. They belong together.

SLIDE 5 — TRACTION
One kitchen. Real tortillas. Zero approvals. Zero templates. Zero per-message fees. Zero crying. Scale is a config file. Brand number forty-seven costs the same as brand number two: thirty minutes, one QR code, three lines of YAML. Growth is copy-paste. The for-loop has never once asked for equity.

SLIDE 6 — COMPETITION
Them: six-week onboarding, $2,000 a month, pre-approved templates, 24-hour reply windows, your data on their cloud, their subprocessors' subprocessors, good luck leaving. Us: four commands, an afternoon, fifty cents, your disk, your rules, goodbye whenever. The delta between these columns is not technology. The delta is rent extraction. Every dollar in their column that is not in ours is money taken from a cook who just wanted to say "sí, claro, en diez minutos."

SLIDE 7 — THE ASK
Nothing. We are not raising. We are not selling. We are not hiring a VP of Growth or attending SaaStr Annual or writing a blog post about the future of conversational anything. We open-sourced the whole thing so the community grinds the edge cases and no cook ever loses an order to a typing speed problem again. The ask is zero dollars and zero percent. The return is every kitchen on earth getting its lunch hour back.

SLIDE 8 — WHY NOW
Because tech died. Because the VCs stole the last idea and called it synergy. Because a taco depends on one variable you control — whether it tastes good — and after eighteen months of variables controlled by other people, that feels like oxygen. Because the most disruptive thing you can build in 2026 is the boring thing every coding book told you to build and every founder tutorial told you to skip.

CLOSING
We have nothing to sell. We have a while-loop, a cook, and a Tuesday lunch rush that does not fall over. The billion-dollar industry calls this unscalable. The cook calls it lunch. We know which opinion we trust.
